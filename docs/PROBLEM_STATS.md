# **NyayaVedika: Scale and Severity of the Problem**

*Code for a Billion submission. Criteria addressed: Size of the problem and Severity of the problem. Prepared 7 November 2026\.*

Every figure carries a source link. Each is tagged **Reported** (published by an agency), **Derived** (arithmetic on reported figures) or **Estimate** (our model, with assumptions stated).

## **Summary**

India's courts hold about **5.9 crore pending cases**. About **4 lakh people in jail are undertrials**, 75.8% of all prisoners, and they have not been convicted of any offence. Roughly **1.2 lakh of them had been in custody for more than a year** at the end of 2023, and about **33,000 for more than three years**.

The law already provides relief. Section 479 of the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita (BNSS) requires release after half the maximum sentence, or one-third for first-time offenders. Even so, in the last quarter of 2025 the national review committees identified only **209** undertrials under the half-sentence category and **96** were released. The binding constraint is identification and follow-through, not the law.

## **1\. Size of the problem**

### **Courts**

* District and subordinate courts held about **5.21 crore** pending cases on 27 September 2026 ([NJDG, via ForThePeople](https://forthepeople.in/en/india/justice-pendency)). Criminal cases outnumber civil cases about 3.4 to 1 there ([Judicio, citing NJDG](https://judicio.ai/blogs/indian-judiciary-pendency-statistics)).  
* The 25 High Courts held about **65 lakh** ([September 2026 review](https://sandeephegde.substack.com/p/do-we-really-know-how-many-cases)).  
* The Supreme Court held about **95,000**, its highest ever ([Judicio, citing the Supreme Court Observer](https://judicio.ai/blogs/indian-judiciary-pendency-statistics)). The Supreme Court itself cited a total of 5.61 crore in a July 2026 judgment.  
* About 10% of district cases and about 24% of High Court cases were over ten years old in July 2026, roughly **64 lakh cases** in all (Derived from the same NJDG snapshot).

Each case involves at least two parties, so the number of people with a case stuck in court is larger than the case count.

In the target states in October 2025, Telangana held 5,238 undertrials (73% of its prisoners) and Andhra Pradesh held 4,370 (67%). Uttar Pradesh held the most, at 67,252 ([NALSA UTRC Q4 2025](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s32e45f93088c7db59767efef516b306aa/uploads/2026/02/20260211666479586.pdf)).

### **Legal aid entitlement**

The India Justice Report states that almost 80% of the population is eligible for free legal aid, including women, children, people in custody, persons with disabilities and Scheduled Castes and Tribes ([IJR legal aid backgrounder](https://indiajusticereport.org/files/IJR_2022_Legal_Aid_Backgrounder.pdf)). On a population of about 144 crore that is roughly 115 crore people (Estimate).

## **2\. Severity: human suffering**

### **Long detention without conviction**

Undertrials in custody at the end of 2023, by time held ([NCRB PSI 2023](https://www.ncrb.mha.gov.in/uploads/files/PSI-2023.pdf)):

| Time in custody | Undertrials | Share |
| :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Up to 1 year | 2,70,256 | 69.3% |
| 1 to 2 years | 54,655 | 14.0% |
| 2 to 3 years | 31,601 | 8.1% |
| 3 to 5 years | 23,006 | 5.9% |
| Over 5 years | 10,392 | 2.7% |

From these bands (Derived): **1,19,654** were held over one year, **64,999** over two years and **33,398** over three years.

### **The release provision exists but is rarely applied**

Section 479 BNSS replaced Section 436A of the Code of Criminal Procedure. It requires release on bail once an undertrial has served half the maximum sentence for the alleged offence. First-time offenders qualify after one-third. It excludes offences punishable by death or life imprisonment and people with multiple pending cases ([Drishti IAS summary](https://www.drishtiias.com/daily-updates/daily-news-analysis/state-of-undertrial-prisoners-in-india/print_manually)).

* NCRB recorded 1,384 undertrials as eligible for premature release under the old Section 436A in 2023 ([NCRB dataset on Dataful](https://dataful.in/datasets/23011)).  
* For 2024, a [preprint citing PSI 2024 Table 7.4](https://www.researchsquare.com/article/rs-10743922/v1) reports 968 identified as eligible, 467 applications moved by jail superintendents and 627 released. We could not tie these to a PSI page and mark them for verification.  
* Against 33,398 undertrials held over three years, the reported number identified as eligible is in the hundreds to low thousands. The figures come from different years, so this shows scale rather than an exact miss rate.

### **What happens after review: NALSA, October to December 2025**

The Under Trial Review Committees meet quarterly in every district. Their Q4 2025 report ([NALSA](https://cdnbbsr.s3waas.gov.in/s32e45f93088c7db59767efef516b306aa/uploads/2026/02/20260211666479586.pdf), Tables D to J) shows how many people drop out at each stage:

| Stage | All categories |
| :---- | :---- |
| Identified for review | 14,387 |
| Recommended for release | 10,754 |
| Applications filed | 9,011 |
| Released | 5,864 (55% of those recommended) |

By category:

| Category | Identified | Recommended | Filed | Released |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| 1: Section 436A / 479 BNSS (half sentence) | 209 | 175 | 164 | 96 |
| 2: First-time offenders at one-third sentence | 279 | 255 | 250 | 209 |
| 3: Bail granted but surety not furnished | 4,345 | 3,455 | 3,085 | 1,936 |

Category 3 means people the court had already granted bail who stayed in jail because they could not furnish a surety. NALSA's report names the non-release of people recommended under statutory-bail categories as a continuing concern. Its own tables print inconsistent release percentages for Category 1 (34% in one table, 55% in a summary), so we cite the counts.

### **Estimate: undertrials who are probably eligible today (Estimate)**

We applied exclusion shares to the reported detention counts above. The shares are our assumptions: *f* is the share charged with offences punishable by death or life imprisonment, and *g* is the share with no other case pending. Neither is published.

| Case | Pool (PSI 2023\) | f | g | Eligible |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Low | 33,398 (held over 3 years) | 45% | 60% | 11,021 |
| Central | 64,999 (held over 2 years) | 35% | 70% | 29,575 |
| High | 1,19,654 (held over 1 year) | 25% | 80% | 71,792 |

We present this as **roughly 10,000 to 70,000 people, with a central case near 30,000**. The pilot will replace *f* and *g* with real jail records.

## **3\. Severity: economic loss**

### **Cost to the state of excess custody (Estimate)**

* NCRB reports ₹2,669.88 crore spent directly on inmates in FY 2023–24 ([PSI 2023](https://www.ncrb.mha.gov.in/uploads/files/PSI-2023.pdf)). Divided by 5,30,333 inmates, that is about **₹50,300 a year, or ₹4,195 a month** per inmate (Derived).  
* Total prison spending was ₹8,834.6 crore (same source), about ₹13,900 per inmate per month including staff and overheads (Derived).  
* We use the lower direct figure to stay conservative.

| Case | Eligible people | Extra months in custody | Cost |
| :---- | :---- | :---- | :---- |
| Low | 11,021 | 3 | ₹13.9 crore |
| Central | 29,575 | 6 | ₹74.4 crore |
| High | 71,792 | 12 | ₹361.4 crore |

The Supreme Court's judgment in [Suhas Chakma v. Union of India](https://aphc.gov.in/docs/circulated_judgements/judgment_WPC_1082_2020.pdf) cites about ₹7,094 a month per prisoner in a closed prison. That is a Rajasthan figure, not a national one. Using it would raise the central case to about ₹126 crore.

Two limits apply. The extra months are an assumption, because we found no data on them. The saving from releasing one person is also lower than the average cost, because buildings and staff remain. These figures exclude lost wages, lost jobs and the cost to families.

### **Cost to litigants**

The [DAKSH Access to Justice Survey](https://www.dakshindia.org/access-to-justice-survey-findings-india) interviewed 9,329 litigants at 305 locations in 24 states. It estimated lost wages and business income at **₹50,387 crore a year, 0.48% of GDP**. It also estimated about [₹30,000 crore a year](https://qz.com/india/670401/justice-must-cry-log-jammed-courts-are-wiping-almost-0-5-off-indias-gdp) in direct litigation spending. The survey was done in 2015–16, so today's cost is likely higher, but we have not scaled the figure.

## **4\. The delivery gap: legal aid reaches few of those entitled**

* 15,50,164 people received free legal aid in FY 2023–24 ([as reported from the India Justice Report 2025](https://pwonlyias.com/current-affairs/legal-aid-system-in-india/)).  
* 16,60,249 people received it from April 2025 to January 2026 ([PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2239463&reg=3&lang=2)).  
* Against about 115 crore people entitled, that is well under **0.2% a year** (Estimate). This compares people assisted with people entitled, not with people who need a lawyer in a given year, so we call it an entitlement-reach gap.  
* National spending on legal aid is about ₹6.46 per person per year ([India Justice Report 2025](https://lawchakra.in/legal-updates/india-justice-report-2025-judiciary/)).  
* The Legal Aid Defence Counsel scheme worked in 680 districts at the end of 2025\. The Law Ministry reports that 2,76,476 undertrials received representation and 59,630 were released in FY 2025–26 up to December 2025 ([PIB, via ANI](https://thenewsmill.com/2026/03/over-16-6-lakh-people-receive-free-legal-aid-in-fy-2025-26-says-law-ministry/)). The definition of release differs from the review-committee count, so the two should not be added.

## **5\. Why this defines NyayaVedika's job**

The evidence points to a specific gap. The statute already entitles tens of thousands of undertrials to release. Review committees identify only a small share of them each quarter. Of those recommended, 45% are still in custody when the quarter ends, and thousands more have bail but cannot furnish a surety.

NyayaVedika targets that gap by checking time served against the maximum sentence, flagging eligible people early, drafting the application and tracking it until release, with human approval before any action that has legal consequences.

## **6\. Data notes and limits**

1. **Live court counts move daily.** The 64 lakh figure for cases over ten years comes from a July 2026 secondary summary of NJDG. Re-check the live dashboard and record the date before relying on it.  
2. **Prison counts differ by agency and date.** NCRB counts at year-end and NALSA counts quarterly. We name the date and agency beside every figure.  
3. **PSI 2024 details** (detention bands and the 968 / 467 / 627 release figures) come from a preprint and press reports. Check them against the NCRB PDF.  
4. **Legal aid entitlement** of almost 80% is the India Justice Report's figure. We saw it through the report's backgrounder and secondary summaries.  
5. **Estimates** rest on assumptions *f*, *g* and extra months in custody. These are not published figures.  
6. **Earlier draft numbers withdrawn.** We removed several figures that did not survive checking against primary sources, including a PSI 2023 prisoner total of 5,25,446 (the NCRB figure is 5,30,333) and a litigant-cost estimate of ₹3.6 lakh crore that we could not verify.
